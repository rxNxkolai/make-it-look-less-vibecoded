Why the baguettes come back at 11:10

Deck A runs hot for bread and drops through the morning. Deck B sits at 190 for pastry and never moves. Nothing can go in until something else comes out, which is the whole shape of the day.

    The focaccia leaves deck A at 09:40 at 220 degrees. Baguettes need 250 with steam, so the deck spends fifty minutes climbing while the second poolish finishes. In at 10:30, out at 10:55, on the shelf at 11:10. That gap is not a choice, it is a thermometer.

    How the Marlowe loaf is made

    It starts the afternoon before. The starter gets its evening feed at eight, peaks around two in the morning, and that is the jar that goes into the mixer. Everything else is waiting.

    Flour is stoneground from a mill in Kent, mostly white with a tenth of wholemeal for the flavour it gives back on the second day. Water goes in at 32 degrees so the dough lands at 26 after mixing. Salt holds back until the autolyse has done its half hour.

    • Flour90% stoneground white, 10% wholemeal
    • Hydration78%, pushed to 80 in winter
    • Starter18% of flour weight, at peak
    • Salt2.1%, in after the autolyse
    • Bulk4 hours at 26 degrees, four folds
    • Retard18 hours at 4 degrees
    • Bake250 falling to 230, 55 minutes, steam for the first 18

    The folds are the only thing we are strict about

    Four of them, forty minutes apart, and then the dough is left alone. Each one is a lift from underneath and a fold over, a quarter turn, four times round the tub. It takes about thirty seconds. Skipping the last fold gives you a flatter loaf with a wetter crumb, which some people prefer and we do not.

    Shaping happens on an unfloured bench so the dough has something to grip. It goes into linen-lined baskets seam up, into the fridge until the small hours, and out of the fridge straight into the oven with one long score down the length at a shallow angle. That score is what makes the ear.

    Everything else is built around that one bake

    The croissants are laminated the previous morning and sleep in the fridge overnight, so they only need proving and a slot on deck B. The rye is scalded, which means half its flour is mixed with boiling water the day before and left to go sweet. The almond croissants are yesterday's croissants, soaked in syrup and filled, which is what they have always been and why they exist.

    Focaccia is the one thing mixed the same morning. Eighty-five percent hydration, twenty-four hours cold if we have the fridge space, and rosemary from the yard behind the shop. When the yard rosemary runs out in February we say so on the board rather than buying it in.
