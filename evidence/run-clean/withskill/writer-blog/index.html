What the Bakery Knows at Four

By Nell Farraday · 23 July 2026 · 18 minutes

The bakery on Henderson Street starts at ten past midnight, which is early enough that the buses have stopped and late enough that nobody calls it morning. Sana lets me in through the yard door and hands me a coat that smells of flour and diesel from the van. The ovens have been on for forty minutes by then and the room sits at the temperature of a mild fever.

She has done this for eleven years. In that time the street above has gained two coffee shops, lost a hardware store, and been dug up three times. None of it reached her. Work that happens at night is invisible in a particular way: not hidden, just unattended. Nobody is there to see it, so nobody thinks it was done.

I asked her once what the hard part was. I had expected her to say the hours, or the heat, or the drive home in the dark. She said it was walking out at six into a street full of people who assumed the bread had always been there.

What follows is one night, told straight through, with the timings she keeps in her head and none of the ones the company keeps on paper. She read it before it went up, and asked for two changes. Both are in.

The Notebook

Short notes, kept nightly
No. 218

A porter at the Royal told me the corridors go quiet after two but the building gets louder. Pipes, lifts, the generator testing itself at four. He knows the schedule by ear and can date a fault to the week it started.

No. 217

Two hours at the fish market and I filled nine pages, none of it usable. The line I wanted came on the bus home, from a woman who was not part of the story: she weighs by hand, and the scale agrees with her.

No. 216

Reading back the 2019 notebooks. I used to ask people what time they started. Now I ask what they do in the first ten minutes. The answers are better and they take longer, which is the whole trick.

No. 215

The bakery has a radio that only holds one station. Nobody has tried to fix it in six years. Sana says the silence would be worse, and that the presenter has been with her longer than any of the staff.

No. 214

For the ports piece: the crane drivers call the night shift the long one, though it runs the same eight hours as the others. Three people used the phrase without prompting. Ask the fourth why.

Colophon

Who, what, and how
Who
Nell Farraday. Before this I spent four years as a night dispatcher for a haulage firm in Immingham, which is where all of it started.
What
Long reporting about work that happens between midnight and six. Roughly one essay a month. Nothing sponsored, nothing written for a brand, no pieces placed by anyone but me.
Method
Everyone named here has read their part before it went up. Corrections run on this page and stay on it. If I got something wrong about your job, write and say so.
Elsewhere
Essays have run in The Harbour Review, Nightshift Quarterly, and Ganglia. A first book, about ports, is half drafted and behind schedule.
